**Mini-Case 1: Female Patient with Menstrual Dysfunction**  
A 32-year-old woman presents with 6 months of irregular menstrual cycles and galactorrhea. Laboratory testing reveals elevated serum prolactin. What is the most common clinical presentation of prolactinomas in premenopausal women?  
A. Oligo/amenorrhea and galactorrhea  
B. Hypogonadism and increased libido  
C. Weight gain and fatigue  
D. Elevated testosterone and hirsutism  
Correct answer: A. Oligo/amenorrhea and galactorrhea [S5]  

Teaching answer: In premenopausal women, the most typical symptoms of hyperprolactinemia due to prolactinomas are oligo/amenorrhea (approximately 90%) and galactorrhea (approximately 80%) [S5]. When both symptoms are present, about 75% of women have hyperprolactinemia [S5].  

Discussion: Prolactinomas are the most common secretory pituitary adenomas and are more prevalent in women during reproductive years [S3]. Elevated prolactin inhibits pulsatile secretion of gonadotropins, leading to amenorrhea and infertility [S5].

**Mini-Case 2: Treatment Choice in a Patient with Drug Intolerance**  
A 45-year-old man with a macroprolactinoma reports worsening erectile dysfunction and has failed to respond to bromocriptine. He now expresses intolerance to dopamine agonists. What is the next-line treatment option for this patient?  
A. High-dose cabergoline  
B. Transsphenoidal surgery  
C. Radiation therapy  
D. Hormone replacement therapy  
Correct answer: B. Transsphenoidal surgery [S2]  

Teaching answer: Transsphenoidal surgery remains an option when medical therapy is neither effective nor well tolerated, particularly in patients with dopamine agonist intolerance or resistance [S6]. Primary surgical resection of microprolactinomas and enclosed macroprolactinomas has similar remission rates to cabergoline [S2].  

Discussion: Dopamine agonists are the drugs of choice for prolactinoma management due to high efficacy and low adverse effects [S1]. When patients fail medical therapy or have intolerance, surgery is a viable alternative [S6].

**Mini-Case 3: Postmenopausal Prolactinoma Behavior**  
A 58-year-old postmenopausal woman with a history of prolactinoma is being evaluated for symptom recurrence. Her serum prolactin levels have remained stable over the past year. What is the typical behavior of prolactinomas after menopause?  
A. Rapid tumor growth and hyperprolactinemia  
B. Stable tumor size and prolactin levels  
C. Increased risk of metastasis  
D. Requirement for immediate radiation therapy  
Correct answer: B. Stable tumor size and prolactin levels [S4]  

Teaching answer: Prolactinomas diagnosed in the reproductive period often exhibit stable tumor behavior and serum prolactin levels after menopause, with treatment typically discontinued due to reduced gonadotropin suppression [S4].  

Discussion: Menopause may decrease prolactin levels, and some women with macroprolactinomas may consider discontinuing dopamine agonists with close follow-up [S3]. Tumor behavior postmenopause is generally stable, with minimal growth [S4].

**Mini-Case 4: Efficacy of Cabergoline in Tumor Shrinkage**  
A 30-year-old woman with a microprolactinoma is being treated with cabergoline. After 6 months of therapy, her serum prolactin levels normalize. What percentage of patients experience tumor shrinkage with cabergoline therapy?  
A. 50%  
B. 60%  
C. 70%  
D. 80%  
Correct answer: D. 80% [S2]  

Teaching answer: Cabergoline causes tumor shrinkage in up to 80% of patients and normalizes prolactin in up to 85% of patients [S2]. Treatment with up to 2·0 mg per week of cabergoline for at least 6 months achieves adenoma shrinkage of at least 30% in most patients [S6].  

Discussion: Cabergoline is the preferred dopamine agonist due to superior efficacy and tolerability compared to bromocriptine [S2]. It is effective in normalizing prolactin and inducing tumor regression [S6].

**Mini-Case 5: Differential Diagnosis in a Patient with Galactorrhea**  
A 28-year-old woman presents with galactorrhea and amenorrhea. Which of the following conditions should be included in the differential diagnosis of hyperprolactinemia?  
A. Hypothyroidism  
B. Liver cirrhosis  
C. Renal failure  
D. All of the above  
Correct answer: D. All of the above [S1]  

Teaching answer: Differential diagnosis of hyperprolactinemia should include hypothyroidism, renal failure, liver cirrhosis, compression of the pituitary stalk, idiopathic hyperprolactinemia, and other pituitary adenomas [S1].  

Discussion: Elevated prolactin can result from physiological, pharmacological, or pathological causes, including pituitary adenomas and stalk compression [S6]. These conditions must be ruled out before diagnosing a prolactinoma [S1].
