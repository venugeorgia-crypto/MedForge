**Prolactinoma – Quick Cheat Sheet** [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]

**Definition**: Prolactinomas are benign pituitary adenomas that secrete excess prolactin, constituting ~40% of all pituitary tumors [S1].  

**Mechanism Chain**: Elevated prolactin → inhibits gonadotropin release → suppresses ovulation and testosterone → causes oligo/amenorrhea, galactorrhea, loss of libido [S5][S6].

**Must-Know Facts**:  
- Most common in women 20–40 years; rare in pediatric or postmenopausal women [S1][S3].  
- Symptoms: oligo/amenorrhea (~90%), galactorrhea (~80%) in women; loss of libido and potency in men [S1][S5].  
- Dopamine agonists (DA) are first-line therapy; cabergoline normalizes prolactin in ~85% and shrinks tumors in ~80% [S2][S6].  
- Surgery is an option for microprolactinomas or DA intolerance; remission rates are comparable to cabergoline [S2][S6].  
- Radiotherapy is reserved for refractory cases [S6].  
- Postmenopausal women may discontinue DA therapy with stable tumor behavior and prolactin levels [S4].  

**Red-Flag Confusions**:  
- Galactorrhea in men is virtually pathognomonic of prolactinoma, not just a symptom [S5].  
- Hyperprolactinemia can be caused by drugs, hypothyroidism, liver disease, or pituitary stalk compression — must rule out these causes [S1].  
- Not all prolactinomas are aggressive; men’s tumors are larger and more aggressive than women’s [S6].  
- Prolactinomas in postmenopause show stable behavior; macroprolactinomas may still grow without treatment [S4].  

**Quick Recall Prompts**:  
- “Women with amenorrhea + galactorrhea → think prolactinoma” [S5].  
- “Cabergoline first — if no response, consider surgery” [S2][S6].  
- “DA safe in pregnancy; stop at conception, monitor tumor growth” [S3].  
- “Postmenopause? Tumor may stabilize — consider tapering DA” [S4].  
- “Children need multidisciplinary care — treat as adults but with special considerations” [S5].
