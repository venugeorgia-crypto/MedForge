# Big picture  
Prolactinomas are the most common functioning pituitary adenomas, accounting for about 40% of all pituitary tumors and approximately half of all pituitary neuroendocrine tumors [S1][S2]. They are benign, hormone-secreting tumors that cause hyperprolactinemia, leading to reproductive disturbances such as amenorrhea and galactorrhea in women and loss of libido and potency in men [S1][S5]. Dopamine agonists are the first-line treatment, with cabergoline being particularly effective, and surgery or radiation is reserved for refractory or aggressive cases [S1][S2][S6]. The condition is more common in women of reproductive age and shows altered behavior after menopause [S3][S4]. [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]

# Core mechanism  
Prolactinomas arise from pituitary lactotroph cells that secrete excessive prolactin due to loss of dopamine inhibition, which normally suppresses prolactin release [S6]. Elevated prolactin inhibits the pulsatile secretion of gonadotropins (LH and FSH), leading to suppression of ovarian and testicular function [S5]. This mechanism underlies the clinical symptoms of menstrual dysfunction and infertility in women and erectile dysfunction in men [S1][S5]. [S5][S6]

# Anatomy/physiology/pathology/pharmacology connections when supported  
Prolactinomas originate in the anterior pituitary and are typically small, with a female predominance in reproductive years [S1][S6]. The tumor mass effect is often minimal, but elevated prolactin levels dominate clinical presentation [S1]. Dopamine agonists (e.g., bromocriptine, cabergoline) mimic dopamine and inhibit prolactin secretion via D2 receptors on lactotrophs, normalizing serum prolactin in up to 85% of patients and causing tumor shrinkage in up to 80% [S2][S6]. Cabergoline, at doses up to 2.0 mg/week for at least 6 months, achieves at least 30% adenoma shrinkage and restores gonadal function in most patients [S6]. Transsphenoidal surgery is effective for small microprolactinomas and macroprolactinomas with enclosed growth, with remission rates comparable to cabergoline [S2][S6]. Radiotherapy is reserved for treatment-refractory cases, such as aggressive or metastatic tumors [S2][S6]. [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]

# Exam traps  
- Do not assume all prolactinomas are large or symptomatic; most are small and asymptomatic until hyperprolactinemia causes symptoms [S1].  
- Do not confuse prolactinoma with other pituitary tumors; it is the most common functioning pituitary adenoma, not the most common pituitary tumor overall [S1][S2].  
- Do not assume dopamine agonists are equally effective in men and women; men tend to have larger, more aggressive tumors [S6].  
- Do not assume that all patients need lifelong therapy; treatment can be tapered or discontinued after menopause with stable tumor behavior [S3][S4].  
- Do not assume that surgery is always superior to medical therapy; both have similar remission rates for microprolactinomas [S2][S6]. [S1][S2][S3][S4][S6]

# Common confusions  
- Prolactinoma is not the same as hyperprolactinemia; hyperprolactinemia can be physiological (e.g., pregnancy, stress) or due to drug use, renal failure, or liver disease, and must be differentiated [S1][S6].  
- Galactorrhea is not specific to prolactinoma; it occurs in 5–10% of normoprolactinemic women and is not diagnostic [S5].  
- Menopause does not eliminate prolactinoma risk; tumors diagnosed postmenopause may still grow or remain stable, and treatment may be adjusted [S3][S4].  
- Cabergoline is not universally superior to bromocriptine; both are effective, but cabergoline has better compliance and efficacy [S2][S6].  
- Tumor size does not always correlate with symptom severity; small tumors can cause significant hyperprolactinemia [S1]. [S1][S5][S6]

# Five-minute recap  
Prolactinomas are the most common functioning pituitary adenomas, primarily affecting women in their reproductive years [S1][S2]. They cause hyperprolactinemia, which suppresses gonadotropin secretion, leading to menstrual dysfunction and infertility in women and sexual dysfunction in men [S5][S6]. Dopamine agonists, especially cabergoline, are first-line therapy, normalizing prolactin in up to 85% of patients and shrinking tumors in up to 80% [S2][S6]. Surgery is effective for small tumors and in cases of intolerance to dopamine agonists [S2][S6]. After menopause, prolactinomas often show stable behavior, and treatment may be discontinued with monitoring [S3][S4]. Radiotherapy is reserved for aggressive or metastatic disease [S2][S6].
