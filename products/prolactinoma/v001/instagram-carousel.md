## Slide 1: Hook  
Prolactinomas are the most common pituitary adenoma, affecting about 40% of all pituitary tumors [S1].  
They primarily impact women aged 20–40 years, causing symptoms like irregular periods and galactorrhea [S1].  
Despite being benign, untreated prolactinomas can lead to infertility and reduced quality of life [S3].  
This carousel explains diagnosis, treatment, and management — all backed by current evidence [S1][S2][S3].

## Slide 2: What Is a Prolactinoma?  
Prolactinomas are benign pituitary tumors that secrete excess prolactin [S1].  
They are the most common functioning pituitary adenoma, with a female predominance in reproductive years [S6].  
Tumors are classified as micro (less than 10 mm) or macro (larger than 10 mm) based on size [S2].  
Positive immunostaining for prolactin, pituitary-specific transcription factor 1, and estrogen receptor α confirms diagnosis [S6].

## Slide 3: Clinical Presentation  
In premenopausal women, oligo/amenorrhea occurs in ~90% of cases and galactorrhea in ~80% [S5].  
When both symptoms are present, about 75% of women have hyperprolactinemia [S5].  
In men, symptoms include loss of libido and potency due to elevated prolactin [S1].  
Galactorrhea in men is virtually pathognomonic of a prolactinoma [S5].

## Slide 4: Differential Diagnosis  
Hyperprolactinemia may result from drugs, hypothyroidism, liver cirrhosis, or renal failure [S1].  
Compression of the pituitary stalk by other pathologies can mimic prolactinoma [S1].  
Idiopathic hyperprolactinemia and other pituitary adenomas must also be ruled out [S1].

## Slide 5: First-Line Treatment  
Dopamine agonists (DAs) are the drug of choice for prolactinoma management [S1][S2].  
Cabergoline is preferred over bromocriptine due to superior efficacy and fewer side effects [S2].  
Cabergoline normalizes prolactin in up to 85% of patients and causes tumor shrinkage in up to 80% [S2].  
Treatment with up to 2.0 mg per week for at least 6 months achieves at least 30% adenoma shrinkage [S6].

## Slide 6: Surgical and Other Options  
Transsphenoidal surgery is an option for patients with DA intolerance or resistance [S6].  
Primary surgical resection of micro- and enclosed macroprolactinomas has remission rates comparable to cabergoline [S2].  
Radiotherapy is reserved for treatment-refractory cases, such as aggressive or metastatic tumors [S2][S6].  
Multidisciplinary management is recommended for optimal outcomes [S6].

## Slide 7: Special Considerations  
In postmenopausal women, prolactinomas often show stable tumor behavior and prolactin levels [S4].  
DA therapy may be discontinued after menopause with close monitoring [S3][S4].  
Breastfeeding is safe postpartum if no significant tumor growth occurred during pregnancy [S3].  
Prolactinomas are associated with decreased quality of life and may affect bone and cardiovascular health [S3].

## Slide 8: Recap  
Prolactinomas are benign pituitary tumors, most common in women 20–40 years old [S1].  
Symptoms include menstrual dysfunction and galactorrhea in women and loss of libido in men [S1].  
Dopamine agonists, especially cabergoline, are first-line therapy [S2][S6].  
Surgery is reserved for DA intolerance or resistance; radiotherapy is for refractory cases [S2][S6].  
Management should be individualized and multidisciplinary, especially in women across life stages [S3][S6].
