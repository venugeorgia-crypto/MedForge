**Slide 1: Hook**  
🎯 *Did you know?*  
The pituitary gland — a pea-sized structure at the base of your brain — is often called the "master control gland" because it regulates hormones that control growth, metabolism, and the function of other glands.  
👉 Learn how it works, what goes wrong, and how we diagnose and treat it.  

---

**Slide 2: Anatomy & Structure**  
The pituitary gland is located in the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone and is attached to the hypothalamus.  
It has two main parts:  
- **Anterior lobe (adenohypophysis)**: derived from embryonic ectoderm; secretes five hormones.  
- **Posterior lobe (neurohypophysis)**: derived from hypothalamic neurons; stores and releases hormones like ADH and oxytocin.  
*Intermediate lobe* (rare in adults) lies between them.  
[S2]  

---

**Slide 3: Blood Supply**  
The pituitary gland receives blood from two main arteries:  
- **Internal carotid artery** → via the posterior communicating artery  
- **Superior sagittal sinus** → via the anterior communicating artery  
Understanding this anatomy is critical for surgical procedures and imaging.  
[S1]  

---

**Slide 4: Hormonal Functions**  
The anterior pituitary produces five key hormones:  
- TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)  
- ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone)  
- FSH & LH (follicle-stimulating and luteinizing hormones)  
- Prolactin  
- GH (growth hormone)  
These regulate metabolism, reproduction, growth, and stress response.  
[S2]  

---

**Slide 5: Pituitary Tumors – Common & Impact**  
Pituitary tumors are common, with a prevalence of ~75–78 per 100,000 people.  
Most are benign (adenomas) and asymptomatic.  
Symptoms include:  
- Headaches  
- Vision problems (due to optic chiasm compression)  
- Nausea/vomiting  
- Hormonal imbalances (e.g., hyperprolactinemia, acromegaly, Cushing’s disease)  
[S3][S4]  

---

**Slide 6: Diagnosis & Treatment**  
Diagnosis involves:  
- Physical exam  
- Laboratory tests (e.g., ACTH, prolactin)  
- Brain MRI (gold standard for imaging)  
Treatment options:  
- **Surgery** (transsphenoidal) – first-line for large or hormone-secreting tumors  
- **Radiation therapy** – when surgery fails  
- **Medications** – e.g., dopamine agonists for prolactinomas  
Treatment is individualized based on tumor size, hormone secretion, and symptoms.  
[S4][S3]  

---

**Slide 7: Clinical Relevance**  
Pituitary tumors can cause serious endocrine disorders:  
- **Acromegaly** (excess GH) → enlarged hands, feet, facial features  
- **Cushing’s disease** (excess ACTH) → weight gain, high blood pressure, diabetes  
- **Hyperprolactinemia** → infertility, galactorrhea  
These conditions require prompt diagnosis and management to prevent long-term complications.  
[S4]

**Slide 8: Recap**  
✅ The pituitary gland is a small but vital endocrine organ regulating growth and hormone balance.  
✅ It has two lobes: anterior (5 hormones) and posterior (ADH, oxytocin).  
✅ Blood supply comes from internal carotid and anterior communicating arteries.  
✅ Tumors are common and may cause hormonal imbalances or vision loss.  
✅ Diagnosis uses MRI and hormone tests; treatment includes surgery, radiation, or medication.
