**Mini-Case 1: Delayed Diagnosis in an Elderly Patient**  

**Question:** A 72-year-old man presents with joint pain, thickened fingers, and increased sweating. His medical history includes hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Laboratory tests show elevated serum IGF-1 and lack of growth hormone suppression after glucose tolerance testing. What is the most likely diagnosis, and why is diagnosis delayed in this age group? [S5]  

**Teaching Answer:** The most likely diagnosis is acromegaly. Diagnosis is often delayed in elderly patients due to the overlap between aging-related symptoms and those of acromegaly, such as joint pain and sweating, and the insidious progression of the disease. [S5]  

**Discussion:** Acromegaly is typically diagnosed in the third to fourth decades, but incidence increases in the elderly due to longer life expectancy. The clinical presentation mimics aging, leading to delayed recognition. [S5]

**Mini-Case 2: Diabetes Mellitus in Acromegaly**  

**Question:** A 55-year-old woman with acromegaly develops hyperglycemia and insulin resistance despite no significant weight gain. What pathophysiological mechanism explains the development of diabetes mellitus in acromegaly, and how does it differ from obesity-related insulin resistance? [S3]  

**Teaching Answer:** Growth hormone directly attenuates insulin signaling and stimulates lipolysis, leading to decreased glucose uptake in peripheral tissues. This insulin resistance occurs independently of obesity and is paradoxically associated with a lean phenotype and reduced body adipose tissue mass. [S3]  

**Discussion:** In acromegaly, insulin resistance is driven by growth hormone exposure, not adiposity. This makes it a unique model of insulin resistance. [S3]

**Mini-Case 3: Treatment Options for Acromegaly**  

**Question:** A 48-year-old man with a macroadenoma is being evaluated for treatment of acromegaly. After transsphenoidal surgery, his growth hormone levels remain elevated. What is the next-line medical therapy, and what is its mechanism of action? [S4]  

**Teaching Answer:** Somatostatin receptor ligands, such as octreotide or lanreotide, are effective as both primary and postoperative treatment. These agents bind to somatostatin receptors on pituitary cells, inhibiting growth hormone secretion. [S4]  

**Discussion:** For patients with macroadenomas who fail to achieve remission after surgery, medical therapy is essential to control growth hormone levels. [S4]

**Mini-Case 4: Cardiovascular Risk in Acromegaly**  

**Question:** A patient with acromegaly has a history of hypertension and elevated lipid levels. Which comorbidity is most strongly associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in acromegaly, and why? [S3]  

**Teaching Answer:** Diabetes mellitus is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in acromegaly due to growth hormone-induced insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation. [S3]  

**Discussion:** The combination of hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance increases cardiovascular risk, making diabetes a key comorbidity in acromegaly. [S3]

**Mini-Case 5: Mortality and Disease Control**  

**Question:** A 60-year-old patient with acromegaly has a high IGF-1 level at diagnosis and elevated growth hormone at last follow-up. How do these factors relate to mortality risk in acromegaly, and what does this imply about disease management? [S6]  

**Teaching Answer:** Higher IGF-1 at diagnosis and elevated growth hormone at last follow-up are independent predictors of mortality in acromegaly. This underscores the importance of early diagnosis and sustained control of growth hormone levels. [S6]  

**Discussion:** Mortality improves with disease control and management of comorbidities, highlighting the need for rigorous monitoring and timely intervention. [S6]
