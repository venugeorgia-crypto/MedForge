# Big picture  
Acromegaly is a chronic endocrine disorder caused by hypersecretion of growth hormone (GH), most commonly due to a pituitary adenoma, leading to elevated insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) levels [S1]. It presents insidiously with progressive physical changes and systemic complications, including metabolic disturbances, cardiovascular disease, and increased cancer risk [S4]. Early diagnosis and treatment are critical due to high morbidity and mortality, especially in older patients [S1][S5]. The disease is often missed for years, contributing to poor outcomes [S2].  

# Core mechanism  
GH hypersecretion drives increased IGF-1 production, which mediates many of the disease’s effects. GH directly impairs insulin signaling in peripheral tissues, promotes lipolysis, and reduces glucose uptake, leading to insulin resistance independent of obesity [S3]. This results in a lean phenotype with reduced adipose tissue mass despite insulin resistance [S3]. The pathophysiology creates a unique metabolic state where diabetes mellitus is common, even in the absence of obesity [S3].  

# Anatomy/physiology/pathology/pharmacology connections when supported  
The pituitary gland is the primary source of GH overproduction, typically via a pituitary adenoma [S1][S4]. Elevated GH and IGF-1 levels are central to diagnosis and monitoring [S4]. Biochemical confirmation requires elevated serum IGF-1 and lack of GH suppression after oral glucose administration [S4]. Imaging, particularly pituitary MRI, is advised to identify the underlying adenoma [S4].  
Treatment options include transsphenoidal surgery (first-line for accessible tumors), somatostatin receptor ligands (e.g., octreotide, lanreotide), cabergoline, and pegvisomant (a GH receptor antagonist) [S4]. Surgery is effective in many but fails to achieve remission in patients with large or invasive macroadenomas [S4].  
Diabetes mellitus is a common complication due to GH-induced insulin resistance, and patients have increased cardiovascular risk [S3].  

# Exam traps  
- Confusing acromegaly with gigantism: gigantism occurs in children before epiphyseal closure, while acromegaly develops in adults after growth plates fuse [S1].  
- Assuming all patients with acromegaly have diabetes: while ~1/3 develop diabetes, it is not universal [S3].  
- Believing acromegaly is rare: it is rare but incidence may be rising in the elderly due to increased life expectancy [S5].  
- Overestimating cancer risk: while long-term GH exposure may promote tumor development, epidemiological evidence remains inconclusive, and the association is debated [S6].  

# Common confusions  
- Acromegaly is not caused by excess GH alone; it is defined by hypersecretion of GH and elevated IGF-1 [S1].  
- GH-induced insulin resistance occurs independently of obesity and is paradoxically linked to low body fat [S3].  
- The disease is not limited to young adults: elderly patients are increasingly diagnosed, and comorbidities are more prevalent in this group [S5].  
- Treatment success is not guaranteed: even after surgery, many patients with macroadenomas remain non-remitted [S4].

# Five-minute recap  
Acromegaly results from GH hypersecretion due to pituitary adenoma, leading to elevated IGF-1 and systemic complications [S1][S4]. GH directly causes insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, even in lean patients [S3]. Diagnosis relies on biochemical testing and MRI [S4]. Treatment begins with surgery, followed by medical therapy if needed [S4]. Diabetes is common and carries cardiovascular risk [S3]. Mortality is linked to age, GH levels, and IGF-1 at diagnosis [S6]. The disease is increasingly seen in the elderly, and delayed diagnosis remains a key challenge [S5].
