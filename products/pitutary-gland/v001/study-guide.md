### # Big Picture  
The pituitary gland is a small, pea-sized endocrine organ at the base of the brain, serving as the "master control gland" by regulating hormone production that influences growth, metabolism, reproduction, and stress response. It is anatomically and functionally linked to the hypothalamus, which controls its hormone secretion. Pituitary tumors are common but often asymptomatic; when they occur, they may disrupt hormone balance, leading to endocrine disorders such as Cushing’s syndrome or hyperthyroidism.  

[S4]

---

### # Core Mechanism  
The pituitary gland receives regulatory signals from the hypothalamus via neurohormones (e.g., TRH, CRH, GnRH, dopamine) that control the release of pituitary hormones. The anterior pituitary secretes five key hormones:  
- TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)  
- ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone)  
- FSH/LH (follicle-stimulating hormone/luteinizing hormone)  
- Prolactin  
- GH (growth hormone)  

These hormones regulate thyroid, adrenal, gonadal, and metabolic functions. The posterior pituitary stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), which are produced in the hypothalamus.

### # Anatomy/Physiology/Pathology/Pharmacology Connections  

**Anatomy:**  
- The pituitary is located in the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone.  
- Divided into anterior (adenohypophysis), posterior (neurohypophysis), and intermediate lobe (small, often vestigial).  
- Hypothalamus regulates anterior pituitary via portal system.  

[S2]  

**Physiology:**  
- Hypothalamic hormones (e.g., TRH → TSH; CRH → ACTH; GnRH → FSH/LH) stimulate anterior pituitary hormone release.  
- Dopamine inhibits prolactin secretion (dopamine agonists used in prolactinoma).  
- Posterior pituitary releases oxytocin (labor, lactation) and vasopressin (fluid balance).  

[S2]  

**Pathology:**  
- Pituitary tumors are common but often asymptomatic.  
- Hormone-producing tumors (e.g., prolactinoma) cause endocrine imbalances:  
  - Prolactinoma → galactorrhea, amenorrhea, infertility [S6]  
  - ACTH-secreting tumors → Cushing’s syndrome [S4]  
- Tumors may compress optic chiasm → vision loss, headaches [S4]  
- Can cause hyperthyroidism via TSH overproduction (rare) [S4]  

**Pharmacology:**  
- Dopamine agonists (e.g., cabergoline) treat prolactinoma by suppressing prolactin.  
- Surgery, radiation, or chemotherapy may treat large or symptomatic tumors.  
- No evidence in provided sources for herbal medicine effects on pituitary function beyond traditional use in HPA/HPG axis diseases.  

[S4][S6][S2]

### # Exam Traps  
❌ **Confusing pituitary with hypothalamus**: The hypothalamus *controls* the pituitary, not the other way around.  
❌ **Assuming all pituitary tumors are hormone-secreting**: Most are non-functional and asymptomatic.  
❌ **Misattributing hormone sources**: Prolactin is produced in anterior pituitary; oxytocin/vasopressin are hypothalamic, stored in posterior pituitary.  
❌ **Overgeneralizing herbal medicine effects**: Only one study (S1) reviews traditional herbal use in HPA/HPG axis diseases — no evidence of efficacy or mechanism.  

---

### # Common Confusions  
- **Prolactin vs. prolactinoma**: Prolactin is a hormone; prolactinoma is a tumor that overproduces it.  
- **ACTH vs. Cushing’s**: ACTH stimulates cortisol; excess ACTH → Cushing’s syndrome.  
- **Posterior vs. anterior pituitary**: Posterior stores hormones from hypothalamus; anterior produces hormones independently.  
- **Tumor size vs. symptoms**: Small tumors may not cause symptoms; symptoms arise from hormone excess or mass effect.

### # Five-Minute Recap  
- Pituitary = "master gland" at base of brain, connected to hypothalamus.  
- Anterior lobe secretes TSH, ACTH, FSH/LH, GH, prolactin.  
- Posterior lobe stores and releases oxytocin and vasopressin.  
- Tumors are common but often silent; symptoms arise from hormone excess or mass effect.  
- Prolactinoma → galactorrhea, amenorrhea, infertility.  
- ACTH-secreting tumors → Cushing’s syndrome.
