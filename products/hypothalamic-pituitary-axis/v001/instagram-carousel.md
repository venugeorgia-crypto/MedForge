## Slide 1: Hook  
Obesity and stress both disrupt key brain-endocrine axes—what happens in the hypothalamus?  
These axes control reproduction, stress response, and behavior—yet they are often overlooked in clinical practice [S1].

## Slide 2: Anatomy of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary Axes  
The hypothalamus links the brain to endocrine glands via two major axes:  
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis regulates female reproduction [S2].  
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis manages stress response [S3].  
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis governs male reproduction and aggression [S4].  

## Slide 3: HPO Axis and Female Fertility  
Ovulation requires a surge of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from hypothalamic neurons [S1].  
Metabolic signals from leptin and other neurons modulate GnRH release via POMC, NPY/AgRP, and nNOS neurons [S1].  
Obesity disrupts this signaling, leading to ovulatory disorders [S1].  

## Slide 4: HPA Axis and Stress Response  
The HPA axis activates during stress, releasing cortisol via adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) [S3].  
Glucocorticoids like cortisol are central to stress adaptation and homeostasis [S5].  
Females show greater HPA axis activation than males under stress, possibly explaining higher rates of stress-related disorders [S3].  

## Slide 5: HPA Axis and Mental Health  
Early life stress (ELS) causes persistent HPA axis dysregulation, altering glucocorticoid receptor function [S6].  
This dysregulation increases risk for adult mental health disorders such as depression and PTSD [S6].  
Chronic stress leads to prolonged cortisol exposure, disrupting physiological balance [S6].  

## Slide 6: Interactions Between HPA and HPG Axes  
The HPA and HPG axes interact in regulating behavior and physiology [S4].  
Stress-induced glucocorticoids can suppress gonadal hormone release, impairing reproductive function [S4].  
Testosterone, via androgen receptors in the brain, contributes to aggressive behavior [S4].  

## Slide 7: Clinical Implications  
- In obesity, hypothalamic inflammation and leptin signaling impair HPO axis function [S1].  
- In stress, HPA axis dysregulation contributes to both mental illness and reproductive dysfunction [S3, S4].  
- Early life stress may permanently alter HPA axis function, increasing lifelong vulnerability [S6].  

## Slide 8: Recap  
The hypothalamus coordinates reproduction and stress through the HPO and HPA axes.  
Obesity disrupts HPO axis function via metabolic and inflammatory pathways [S1].  
Stress dysregulates HPA axis, with sex-specific responses and long-term consequences [S3, S6].  
Understanding these axes is key to treating reproductive and mental health disorders [S2, S4].
